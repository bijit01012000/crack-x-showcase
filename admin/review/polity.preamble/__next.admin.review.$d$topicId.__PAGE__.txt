14:T650,**Sovereign** means that India is not a dependency or dominion of another country. No authority stands above the Indian State, and it manages its internal and external affairs freely. Until the Indian Independence Act of 1947 India was a colony; from 15 August 1947 to 26 January 1950 it was a dominion; it stopped being a dominion on 26 January 1950 by becoming a sovereign republic. Two points are often tested. India's continued membership of the Commonwealth, with the British Crown accepted as its symbolic head, does not dilute sovereignty, because the link is voluntary and can be ended at will. Membership of the United Nations (India joined in 1945) is likewise no limit on sovereignty. As a sovereign State, India may acquire foreign territory or cede its own to another State.

**Socialist** entered the text only in 1976, but the idea was already present through several Directive Principles, so the amendment made explicit what had been implicit. The Congress had resolved at its Avadi session in 1955 to work for a socialistic pattern of society. The Indian model is **democratic socialism**: a mixed economy in which public and private sectors operate together. It differs from state socialism, which nationalises all means of production and ends private property. The Supreme Court has described it as aiming to end poverty, ignorance, disease and inequality of opportunity, and as a blend of Marxism and Gandhism that leans towards the Gandhian side. The economic reforms of 1991 (liberalisation, privatisation, globalisation) are widely seen as having softened the socialist character of the State.15:T7b7,**Secular** also came through the 42nd Amendment. Even before that, the Supreme Court said in 1974 that the framers plainly meant to create a secular State, and that Articles 25 to 28, which protect freedom of religion, show it. India follows the **positive** idea of secularism: every religion, whatever its numbers, has equal standing and receives equal support from the State. States can be grouped by their stance on religion.

| Type of State | Attitude to religion | Example given |
|---|---|---|
| Atheistic | Opposes all religion | – |
| Theocratic | Prefers one religion as the State religion | Pakistan, Sri Lanka |
| Secular | Neutral; no State religion | India, USA |

**Democratic** means that the highest power belongs to the people. Democracy may be direct or indirect. In direct democracy, as in Switzerland, people rule themselves through devices such as **referendum, initiative, recall and plebiscite**. In indirect or representative democracy, elected representatives run the government and make laws; this form may be parliamentary or presidential. India has a representative parliamentary system, where the executive answers to the legislature. Its marks include adult franchise, regular elections, the rule of law, an independent judiciary and non-discrimination.

The Preamble uses the word broadly, covering social and economic democracy as well as political. Dr Ambedkar made the point on 25 November 1949: political democracy cannot last without social democracy beneath it, and liberty, equality and fraternity form a union, so that separating any one from the others defeats the purpose of democracy.

| Device | What it allows |
|---|---|
| Referendum | A proposed law is put to the electorate for a direct vote |
| Initiative | People themselves propose a bill to the legislature |
| Recall | Voters remove a representative before the term ends |
| Plebiscite | Public opinion is sought on an important question, often a territorial dispute |16:T92b,**Justice** has three forms. Social justice means equal treatment without distinction of caste, colour, race, religion or sex, and better conditions for backward classes and women. Economic justice means no discrimination on economic grounds and the narrowing of glaring gaps in wealth, income and property. Social and economic justice together are called **distributive justice**. Political justice means equal political rights, equal access to office and an equal voice in government. The triad is said to be borrowed from the Russian Revolution of 1917.

**Liberty** means freedom from restraint combined with opportunity to develop one's personality. The Preamble lists liberty of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship, which the Fundamental Rights make enforceable in court. It is not licence: it is qualified by limits in the Constitution itself. Liberty, equality and fraternity are said to come from the French Revolution (1789–1799).

**Equality** means no special privileges for any section and adequate opportunity for all. Three dimensions are present.

| Dimension | Provisions |
|---|---|
| Civic | Article 14 (equality before law), 15 (no discrimination on religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth), 16 (public employment), 17 (untouchability abolished), 18 (titles abolished) |
| Political | Article 325 (no one excluded from electoral rolls on grounds of religion, race, caste or sex) and Article 326 (adult suffrage for Lok Sabha and Assembly elections) |
| Economic | Article 39 (Directive Principle): adequate livelihood for men and women alike, equal pay for equal work |

**Fraternity** means a feeling of brotherhood. The Constitution encourages it through single citizenship and through the Fundamental Duty in Article 51A to promote harmony among all people and to give up practices that demean women. The Preamble says fraternity must secure the **dignity of the individual** and the **unity and integrity of the nation**; "integrity" was added in 1976. K. M. Munshi explained dignity as recognising every person as sacred. Unity and integrity cover both psychological and territorial national integration. Article 1 calls India a Union of States, signalling that states cannot secede and that the Union is indestructible. The aim is to overcome communalism, regionalism, casteism, linguism and secessionism.17:T50d,The courts moved through three stages.

| Case | Holding |
|---|---|
| Berubari Union (1960) | The Preamble is a key to the minds of the makers and can help to interpret ambiguous articles, but it is **not** a part of the Constitution |
| Kesavananda Bharati (1973) | Overruled that view: the Preamble **is** part of the Constitution and the whole document is to be read in its light |
| LIC of India (1995) | Again held that it is an integral part |

The Preamble was enacted by the Constituent Assembly like the rest, but only after the other provisions had been passed, so that it would match them. The Assembly's President put the question that the Preamble stand part of the Constitution, and it was adopted, so the present judicial view matches the founders' intent. Two limits remain: the Preamble neither confers power on the legislature nor restricts it, and it is **non-justiciable**, meaning it cannot be enforced in court.

On amendability, the question arose for the first time in Kesavananda Bharati. The Court held that the Preamble can be amended under Article 368 provided the **basic features** are not altered, since the Constitution's edifice rests on the basic elements named in the Preamble.
