Fundamental Rights

UPSC CSEOPSC OCS· 6 min readStatus: DRAFT

Layout sample written by the engineering lead to preview the note, card and practice formats. Not fact-checked; will be replaced by an Editor packet.

Objective · Know the six categories of Fundamental Rights, which articles they sit in, and the limits and remedies that examiners most often test.

Fundamental Rights are in Part III of the Constitution (Articles 12–35). They are justiciable: a person can go to court when they are violated.

The six categories

CategoryArticles
Right to Equality14–18
Right to Freedom19–22
Right against Exploitation23–24
Right to Freedom of Religion25–28
Cultural and Educational Rights29–30
Right to Constitutional Remedies32

The Constitution originally had seven categories. The Right to Property (Article 31) was removed by the 44th Amendment Act, 1978 and is now a constitutional right under Article 300A, not a Fundamental Right.

Source:The Constitution of India (as amended)

Article 19 — freedoms of citizens

Article 19 guarantees six freedoms to citizens only: speech and expression, peaceful assembly without arms, forming associations or unions, free movement throughout India, residence and settlement anywhere in India, and practising any profession, occupation, trade or business.

Each freedom can be limited by reasonable restrictions that the State imposes by law on the grounds listed in Article 19(2)–(6).

Source:The Constitution of India (as amended)

Life, liberty and education

Article 21 protects life and personal liberty against deprivation except by procedure established by law. It is available to citizens and non-citizens alike.

Article 21A, added by the 86th Amendment Act, 2002, makes free and compulsory education a Fundamental Right for children aged 6 to 14.

Source:The Constitution of India (as amended)

Remedies and emergencies

Article 32 lets a person move the Supreme Court directly to enforce Fundamental Rights. The Court can issue five writs: habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari and quo warranto. High Courts have a wider writ power under Article 226.

During a national emergency, the enforcement of most Fundamental Rights can be suspended. After the 44th Amendment, Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended.

Source:The Constitution of India (as amended)

Revise in 60 seconds

  • Part III, Articles 12–35; justiciable.
  • Six categories now; Right to Property moved to Article 300A by the 44th Amendment (1978).
  • Article 19 has six freedoms, for citizens only, subject to reasonable restrictions.
  • Article 21 covers everyone; Article 21A (86th Amendment, 2002) covers education for ages 6–14.
  • Article 32 goes to the Supreme Court with five writs; Article 226 gives High Courts wider writ power.
  • Articles 20 and 21 survive an emergency.

Common exam traps

  • "Right to Property is a Fundamental Right": false since 1978.
  • "Article 19 applies to foreigners": false; it is for citizens only.
  • "Article 32 writs can be issued only for Fundamental Rights, and so can Article 226 writs": Article 226 also reaches other legal rights.
